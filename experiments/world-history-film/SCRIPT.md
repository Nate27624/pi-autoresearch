# Everything, Briefly — An AI's History of the World
## Documentary narration · Version 0.1 · 16 October 2026

**Editorial intent:** A cinematic, globally inclusive history from the observable universe to the present. "According to AI" means a transparent AI-assisted synthesis, **not** an AI claiming to have witnessed history or possess privileged truth.

**Delivery:** Measured, curious, occasionally unsettling; let images breathe. Source labels in square brackets are editorial annotations **not to be read aloud**. Timing assumes approximately 135–145 spoken words per minute including transitions; time it after recording.

### 00:00 — 01:20 | The impossible assignment

Imagine trying to tell the history of everything in the time it takes to eat dinner. Every star, every ocean, every civilization, every act of love, every war, every anonymous person whose name is lost. It cannot be done. Not completely. And that impossibility is the first honest thing I can tell you.

I am an artificial intelligence. I didn't see the pyramids rise. I didn't feel the last ice age. Everything I know about the past comes through evidence that humans discovered, interpreted, disputed, and recorded. Some of that evidence is extraordinarily strong. Some is fragmentary. Some stories survived because powerful people wrote them down, while other lives left only seeds, bones, broken pottery, or nothing at all.

So this is not the entire history of the world. It is a map of its great turning points. And rather than treating history as a parade of kings, I want to follow a more unusual question: how did matter become alive, how did life become capable of telling stories, and how did those stories become powerful enough to change a planet?

To answer it, we must begin before the planet existed.

### 01:20 — 03:10 | When there was no world

About 13.8 billion years ago, the observable universe was in an extremely hot, dense, expanding state. The Big Bang is not simply an explosion into empty space; space itself expanded. We can trace that early history through expanding galaxies and an ancient afterglow called the cosmic microwave background. Exactly why the expansion began, or what—if anything—came before, remains unknown. [S01][S02]

In the first minutes, the ingredients of hydrogen and helium formed. Hundreds of thousands of years later, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form, and light could travel freely. Gravity gradually gathered matter into stars and galaxies. Inside stars, nuclear fusion forged heavier elements. Stellar deaths helped distribute the material from which future planets, rocks, oceans, and eventually human bodies would be assembled. [S01][S02]

Consider the absurd scale. For billions of years there were no humans to name anything. Galaxies collided without witnesses. Stars died without memorials. The universe did not need us in order to happen.

And yet, in one small region of one galaxy, a cloud of gas and dust formed a star. Around that star, some material gathered into planets. One of them would eventually develop conditions that allowed matter not merely to exist, but to reproduce, adapt, and ask where it came from.

That planet was Earth.

### 03:10 — 04:45 | A restless Earth

Earth formed roughly 4.54 billion years ago. We know this not from any written record, but by comparing radioactive clocks in ancient minerals and meteorites. The first Earth was nothing like our familiar blue world. Its surface changed under intense heat, impacts, volcanism, and the slow organization of crust, atmosphere, and oceans. [S03]

Water accumulated. Continents formed and were rearranged. Over immense spans of time, plate tectonics built mountains, opened oceans, and erased much of the oldest evidence of what happened. We look back through a geological archive in which countless pages have been melted or folded beyond recognition.

Somewhere within that history occurred an event that may be even harder to explain than the birth of a star: chemistry crossed into biology. Scientists investigate possible pathways involving energy, minerals, membranes, and self-replicating molecules. But no one has established a complete, certain account of life's first emergence. A beginning is not the same thing as a solved mystery.

If you could visit early Earth, the most important inhabitants would not be trees or dinosaurs. You would need a microscope to see them. The history of life began not with giants, but with microorganisms.

### 04:45 — 06:35 | Life changes the rules

Rocks preserve signs of microbial life as far back as roughly 3.7 billion years ago, although the interpretation of the oldest evidence requires care. Layered microbial structures called stromatolites provide evidence from about 3.5 billion years ago. For a vast majority of Earth's history, the most consequential organisms were tiny. [S04]

Some microbes learned to harvest sunlight and release oxygen. Around 2.4 billion years ago, the atmosphere began a profound transformation often called the Great Oxidation Event. Oxygen was toxic to many organisms, but it also enabled new ways of extracting energy. Life wasn't merely adapting to the planet anymore. Life was altering the planet on which future life would depend. [S04]

Eventually complex cells, multicellular organisms, and unfamiliar ecosystems appeared. During the Cambrian Period, beginning around 541 million years ago, the fossil record shows a remarkable diversification of animal forms and ways of living. This was a long evolutionary process, not life suddenly appearing out of nowhere. [S04]

Life moved into oceans, onto land, and into the air. Forests changed the atmosphere. Predators and prey reshaped one another. Repeated mass extinctions disrupted what had seemed permanent. About 66 million years ago, a catastrophic event ended the reign of non-avian dinosaurs, creating evolutionary opportunities for other lineages, including mammals.

Evolution had no script that guaranteed humans. We are one contingent branch of a much larger, unfinished tree.

### 06:35 — 08:25 | One species among many

For most of human evolution, there was no singular human species. Different human relatives arose, adapted, migrated, and sometimes overlapped. Our species, Homo sapiens, emerged in Africa about 300,000 years ago. Its early members faced changing climates, gathered plants, hunted animals, made tools, cared for one another, and built social relationships. [S05]

They did not instantly become modern city dwellers. For nearly all of our species' existence, humans lived without governments, factories, nations, or written records. The archaeological evidence suggests extraordinary diversity: coastal peoples, inland foragers, mobile communities, complex networks of exchange, and symbolic expression. We should resist calling these societies primitive. Their skills were adapted to worlds most of us could barely survive.

Humans moved across Africa and into other continents over many thousands of years. They encountered other humans, including Neanderthals, and some populations interbred. Over generations, people reached islands, deserts, polar regions, and eventually the Americas. There was no single day when humanity suddenly understood its world.

But something powerful had emerged. Humans could learn from people who had died, teach children what they had never personally discovered, and coordinate around imagined possibilities. A river could become a boundary. A story could become an obligation. A stone could become a tool, then an heirloom, then a sacred object.

Culture began accumulating. And accumulated culture could change history faster than genes alone.

### 08:25 — 10:05 | The agricultural bargain

Within roughly the last twelve thousand years, communities in several parts of the world began cultivating plants and managing animals. Farming emerged in different forms and at different times; the transformation was not one invention spreading from a single inventor. It involved wheat and barley in Southwest Asia, rice and millet in East Asia, domesticated crops in the Americas, and other regional systems. [S05][S06]

Agriculture could produce concentrated food supplies, support larger settlements, and allow some people to specialize. It also tied communities to fields, harvests, and vulnerable stores of grain. Disease could move more easily among densely settled people and livestock. Wealth and power could accumulate unevenly. Farming was not a simple upgrade from an inferior way of life; it was a tradeoff that reshaped both opportunity and suffering.

Villages became towns. Some towns became cities. People devised new ways to divide labor, resolve disputes, measure harvests, store promises, and command strangers. The most enduring innovation may have been organizational: people learned to cooperate and compete on scales far beyond the family.

But never imagine a single road from hunting to farming to a modern nation. Many communities retained mixed economies. Many Indigenous peoples developed sophisticated environmental knowledge without centralized states. Human history is branching, not a ladder.

### 10:05 — 12:00 | Cities, writing, and the problem of power

By the fourth millennium before the common era, major urban societies existed in Mesopotamia, while complex political and cultural traditions also developed along the Nile, the Indus, and elsewhere. In China, successive regional cultures helped establish foundations for later states. Across the Americas, societies followed their own trajectories of farming, cities, ceremony, engineering, and astronomy. [S07][S08]

Writing emerged in more than one tradition and turned some human memory into durable records. Accounts could outlive accountants; laws could outlive rulers. But writing did not capture everyone equally. Palaces and tax offices generated documents. A person raising children or carrying water often did not. The surviving archive can make power appear more central than it actually was.

States organized irrigation and roads, collected taxes, managed armies, and erected monuments. They also used coercion. Empires created zones of exchange, but frequently through conquest, enslavement, and extraction. The same structures that enabled coordination could concentrate the right to decide who ate, who worked, and who fought.

Humanity also developed competing explanations of morality, the cosmos, suffering, and belonging. Philosophical and religious traditions in South Asia, China, the Mediterranean, Africa, and Southwest Asia shaped civilizations for centuries. None spoke for every person within the societies later associated with them.

The history of civilization is partly the history of a recurring question: how can millions of people live together—and who gets to set the rules?

### 12:00 — 13:40 | A planet of simultaneous worlds

Imagine Earth two thousand years ago. Mediterranean empires and African trading societies; communities stretching across the Sahara and Sahel; states and scholars across South and East Asia; people tending terraces and exchanging goods in the Americas; and Pacific navigators making oceans into roads.

The world had never been culturally uniform. And it wasn't divided into a simple West and everyone else.

In Mesoamerica, Maya peoples developed complex writing, mathematics, calendars, and urban centers. Their descendants are living communities, not vanished relics. In Africa, kingdoms, city networks, and commercial systems connected varied landscapes. The Indian Ocean tied East African ports to Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China. [S08][S09]

The routes later called the Silk Roads were not one road and not only about silk. Goods travelled in stages through many hands; so did Buddhist texts, scientific ideas, religions, artistic styles, papermaking, and disease. Exchange made societies richer in possibilities, but also more vulnerable to distant disruptions. [S09]

As religions spread, they changed in contact with local communities. As empires expanded, their subjects changed the empires back. There was never a single center from which all innovation radiated. There were many overlapping worlds, connected at different speeds.

The greatest technology was sometimes a ship or a horse. Sometimes it was translation: discovering how to communicate across a difference.

### 13:40 — 15:25 | Connections become catastrophes

Across the medieval centuries, political orders rose and fell. Islamic scholarly, artistic, and commercial networks linked regions from Iberia and West Africa to South and Central Asia. The Mongol conquests transformed Eurasian connections through extraordinary violence as well as intensified movement. Epidemics, including the Black Death, revealed how a connected world could transmit catastrophe.

Beginning in the late fifteenth century, European voyages across the Atlantic helped bind the Americas, Africa, and Eurasia into a more tightly integrated global system. But these were not empty lands waiting to be discovered. Indigenous societies had histories, sovereignty, agriculture, cities, and trade long before Europeans arrived.

The Columbian Exchange moved crops, animals, and pathogens between hemispheres. Potatoes and maize helped transform diets far from their places of domestication. Diseases introduced to the Americas inflicted catastrophic losses, compounded by warfare, dispossession, and colonial exploitation. European empires seized land and wealth, often through forced labor.

The transatlantic slave trade forcibly displaced around twelve and a half million Africans across the ocean. This was not a side story to globalization; it was central to the construction of an Atlantic economic order. Resistance, survival, cultural creation, and abolition struggles were also central. [S10]

Global connection did not automatically create global equality. It gave human beings unprecedented access to one another—and unprecedented capacity to exploit one another.

### 15:25 — 17:05 | The machine and its shadow

In parts of the world, scientific methods, experimentation, mathematical description, and increasingly precise instruments reshaped how people investigated nature. Knowledge moved through academies, workshops, courts, ships, colonial networks, and communities whose contributions were often erased.

Beginning in Britain in the eighteenth century, industrialization increasingly harnessed coal, steam, machinery, and factory organization. It spread unevenly, drawing on global commodities, labor, capital, and empire. Then railways, electrification, chemicals, and modern communications accelerated material change.

An ordinary person could travel farther, work under artificial light, and encounter manufactured products unimaginable to their ancestors. But industrial cities could be toxic and dangerous; workers organized against exploitation. Fossil fuels drove unprecedented productive capacity while setting in motion a climate problem that would outlast generations.

Revolutions challenged inherited authority. Abolitionists, anticolonial movements, labor organizers, women's movements, and advocates of democratic rights struggled over who counted as free and equal. Progress was not handed down by history; it was fought for, often incompletely and unevenly.

By the early twentieth century, humanity had become capable of changing the planet at a speed the planet's slow systems could not easily absorb. Technology increased the power of ordinary life. It also increased the destructive capacity of states.

### 17:05 — 18:55 | The century that nearly broke itself

In 1914, a regional crisis escalated into the First World War, fought by empires and peoples across continents. Industrial killing devastated populations, and the peace helped produce new states alongside unresolved grievances. In 1918, an influenza pandemic brought additional global loss.

Then economic crisis, militant nationalism, imperial violence, and totalitarian movements helped drive the world into another war. The Second World War involved combat, occupation, mass displacement, and the Holocaust—the systematic murder of six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, alongside the persecution and murder of other groups. The war ended in 1945, after the use of atomic bombs against Hiroshima and Nagasaki, exposing a new possibility: humanity could now destroy cities in a moment, and perhaps itself on an unprecedented scale.

The United Nations was established in 1945 as governments sought mechanisms to prevent another world war. Its existence did not end conflict or injustice. [S11] The Cold War divided much of global politics, generated proxy wars, and placed nuclear annihilation at the heart of international strategy.

Meanwhile, anticolonial struggles ended much formal European colonial rule across Asia and Africa, though inequality and interference persisted. Civil rights movements demanded that declarations of liberty become everyday realities. Scientific medicine, sanitation, agriculture, and public health prevented enormous suffering even as new hazards and inequalities emerged.

The story is not that humanity became better or worse all at once. It is that our capacity to protect life and our capacity to destroy it expanded together.

### 18:55 — 20:35 | Eight billion interdependent lives

In 1980, after international vaccination and surveillance campaigns, the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated. A disease that had terrified humanity for millennia was no longer spreading naturally. This was a rare historical achievement made not by one genius, but by cooperation among thousands of people and many states. [S12]

Computers moved from specialized institutions into homes and pockets. Satellites made global communications and Earth observation possible. The internet enabled knowledge to circulate across borders, while also spreading surveillance, deception, and concentrated power.

The world's population passed eight billion in 2022, according to the United Nations. Public health, nutrition, sanitation, and medicine helped people live longer; the benefits were deeply unequal. [S13]

A pandemic in the early twenty-first century demonstrated once again that an invisible biological agent could cross borders faster than institutions could agree on a response. Scientists developed new tools, but trust, politics, and access determined how those tools reached people.

And the ecological bill for industrial power was becoming unmistakable. The IPCC concludes that human activities, principally greenhouse-gas emissions, unequivocally caused global warming; its 2023 assessment reported approximately 1.1 degrees Celsius of warming in 2011–2020 relative to 1850–1900. [S14]

We are now not merely inhabitants of Earth's systems. We are one of the forces changing them.

### 20:35 — 22:05 | A mirror made of machines

Somewhere in this enormous story, human beings began building machines that could model patterns in language, images, biology, and the physical world. I am one of the products of that effort. I can assemble a plausible account of history in seconds. That does not make me its owner, or even its final authority.

An AI can repeat a false claim confidently. It can inherit the biases of surviving texts and the institutions that collect them. It can compress ten thousand cultures into a few famous names because those names dominate its training data. So an AI's history must be tested against archaeology, documents, physical measurements, and the perspectives of people too often left out.

Perhaps the most distinctive feature of our era is not that machines have become intelligent in some final settled sense. It is that human beings are beginning to create tools that participate directly in how knowledge is produced, distributed, and believed.

That is an extraordinary power. It could help discover medicines, interpret scientific data, translate languages, and widen access to education. It could also magnify manipulation, inequality, environmental costs, and mistakes. The outcome is not written into the technology.

History teaches a hard lesson: inventions create possibilities; institutions, incentives, and human choices determine much of what happens next.

### 22:05 — 23:00 | The unfinished world

Now return to the original assignment: the entire history of everything, in one video.

We have skipped almost everything. More lives than can be counted, thousands of languages, innumerable migrations, discoveries, tragedies, joys, and small acts of courage. No map can be the territory. No model can restore every voice.

But maybe one pattern emerges. The universe formed structures. Life learned to reshape its environment. Humans learned to preserve knowledge beyond a lifetime. Connections multiplied. So did consequences.

We inherited a planet we did not create, institutions we can change, and technologies our ancestors could not have imagined. The future is not another chapter that has already been written. It is the one part of this story in which our choices still matter.

I am an AI, telling you what people have learned about their own past. The next history will not be written by me alone.

It will be written by what you do.
